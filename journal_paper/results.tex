\section{Results}\label{results}

This section reports the evidence produced by the proposed benchmarking pipeline under the selected experimental instantiation described in Section~\ref{experiments}. The primary reading is corpus-specific rather than global: for \texttt{education\_eng}, \texttt{M1} is both the highest-\texttt{MAP@10} and lowest-latency candidate; for \texttt{education\_tr}, \texttt{M5} is the quality-first candidate and \texttt{M3} is the lower-latency compromise; and for \texttt{law\_tr}, \texttt{M5} is again quality-first while \texttt{M3} provides the lower-latency Turkish alternative. These recommendations are conditional on the frozen five-model roster, synthetic queries, LLM judgments, raw-L2 retrieval configuration, and compound single-machine timing measurements described in Section~\ref{experiments}. They demonstrate how to interpret one configured benchmark run; they are not default recommendations supplied by the pipeline for other corpora or parameter settings. Because the pipeline generates full benchmark reports automatically, only a selected subset of the most decision-relevant tables and figures is included here; the complete generated reports and supporting files are available through the public repository for interested readers \citep{GitHub_RagRetrievalBench}.

\subsection{Corpus-Specific Selection Outcomes}\label{cross-dataset-headline-findings}

The reported three-dataset snapshot first supports separate dataset-level readings. Table~\ref{tbl:dataset_result_summary} summarizes the corpus-specific quality outcomes: \texttt{M1} leads the English education corpus by \texttt{MAP@10}, while \texttt{M5} leads both Turkish corpora. The timing tables in Sections~\ref{results-education-eng}--\ref{results-law-tr} complete the practical interpretation by showing that \texttt{M1} is also the lowest-latency candidate for \texttt{education\_eng}, whereas \texttt{M3} is the lower-latency compromise among the two Turkish shortlist candidates. A practitioner reading these reports for one organization could therefore select \texttt{M1} for an English education corpus, use \texttt{M5} where Turkish retrieval quality is the priority, or use \texttt{M3} where Turkish latency matters more. The different English/Turkish choices illustrate why one model should not be assumed across all corpora, while the similar Turkish shortlists show that local evaluation can also confirm stability under a shared protocol.

\subsection{Language-Associated Comparison of the Education Corpora}\label{language-effect}

Under the shared evaluation setup in Section~\ref{shared-evaluation-setup}, the English and Turkish education corpora provide a within-snapshot comparison of whether model behavior differs between the two language-associated cases. Because the comparison rests on a single corpus per language, language is confounded with corpus identity, source selection, and qrels composition; the comparison is therefore a bounded case observation rather than an isolated causal estimate. Table~\ref{tbl:language_effect_comparison} shows that the English results sit in a relatively narrow high band, with all five models between 0.3557 and 0.5260 in \texttt{MAP@10}, whereas the Turkish results are lower and more spread by model family.

The English-oriented models (\texttt{M1}, \texttt{M2}, and \texttt{M4}) all decline substantially on \texttt{education\_tr}: \texttt{M1} drops by 0.4029, \texttt{M2} by 0.4082, and \texttt{M4} by 0.2733. By contrast, the multilingual models are more stable across the two education corpora, each dropping only about 0.06 (\texttt{M3} by $0.0585$ and \texttt{M5} by $0.0640$). The evidence therefore suggests a language-associated model-family pattern within this snapshot rather than a universal rule, and it also shows that \texttt{M1} leads \texttt{education\_eng}, so an English-oriented model can still be the strong candidate when the corpus language and retrieval priority align with that model family.

The practical implication is bounded but useful. For local retrieval deployment, language should be treated as a corpus characteristic worth benchmarking separately: the \texttt{MAP@10}-leading candidate for an English corpus is not necessarily the quality-first candidate for a Turkish corpus, even when both corpora are evaluated under the same workflow. That is the main value of this comparison for the paper's fit-for-purpose claim.

\subsection{Quality-Speed Tradeoff}\label{quality-speed-tradeoff}

Table~\ref{tbl:quality_speed_summary} combines secondary aggregate quality with compound timing evidence, and Figure~\ref{fig:results_quality_speed_tradeoff} makes the same tradeoff visible as a two-dimensional ranking. The points form three useful regimes. \texttt{M5} occupies the quality-leading end of the secondary aggregate view with the highest mean \texttt{MAP@10} at 0.3810, but it does so at a weighted latency of 42.94 ms/query and a throughput range of 13.3--13.9 chunks/s. \texttt{M1} anchors the speed-leading end with the lowest weighted latency at 16.28 ms/query and the highest throughput range of 42.3--51.3 chunks/s, but its mean \texttt{MAP@10} is 0.2536 because it performs poorly on the Turkish corpora. \texttt{M2} is the weakest aggregate choice in this snapshot because it combines the lowest mean \texttt{MAP@10} (0.2408) with the highest weighted latency (51.66 ms/query) and the slowest throughput range (3.5--7.7 chunks/s).

Within this roster, the multilingual models \texttt{M3} and \texttt{M5} provide the strongest secondary aggregate retrieval quality, while low latency is concentrated in the lightweight MiniLM models (\texttt{M1}, and to a lesser extent \texttt{M3} and \texttt{M4}); speed is not a pure family pattern, because the English-oriented MPNet model \texttt{M2} is the slowest model overall. This pattern should not be treated as universal: \texttt{M1} leads \texttt{education\_eng}, showing that an English-oriented model can still be an effective choice when the corpus and deployment priority align.

The middle of the plot contains \texttt{M3} and \texttt{M4}, the most plausible compromise candidates when neither extreme is acceptable. \texttt{M3} is the stronger of the two on aggregate retrieval quality, with mean \texttt{MAP@10} of 0.3051 versus 0.2799, at a similar latency (25.05 ms/query versus 26.91), and the two share an average rank of 3.00. The practical reading is therefore conditional: \texttt{M5} is the quality-first aggregate candidate, \texttt{M1} is the speed-first aggregate candidate and the leading candidate for \texttt{education\_eng}, and \texttt{M3} is the lower-latency compromise for the Turkish corpora. On this evidence, no single model leads all corpus-specific and operational priorities jointly; the preferred embedding model depends on whether the practitioner values retrieval effectiveness, indexing cost, or interactive response time more strongly for the corpus at hand.

\subsection{Statistical Decision Support}\label{statistical-decision-support}

The experiment generated 150 queries, but the evaluator scores only positive-bearing queries; after excluding two zero-positive \texttt{education\_eng} queries, the statistical summaries use 148 scored queries, as defined in Table~\ref{tbl:dataset_statistics_summary}. These intervals are descriptive and snapshot-conditional. \subsection{Robustness to Candidate-Pool Construction}\label{robustness-pooled-qrels}

The relevance judgments used throughout this paper are built from a model-independent candidate pool: for each query the top-20 candidates retrieved by all five models are unioned, deduplicated, and judged by the LLM ($9{,}296$ query--chunk pairs across the three datasets, with explicit positive and negative labels). This design reduces the privilege given to any single evaluated model when constructing qrels. To probe sensitivity to this choice within the available frozen evidence, we compare against a single-model candidate pool that uses only \texttt{M5}, an evaluated model, to retrieve the candidates, which was the construction used in the earlier version of this snapshot.

Two findings follow. First, the model-independent pool surfaces substantially more relevant chunks than the single-model pool---312 versus 215 positives on \texttt{education\_eng}, 354 versus 199 on \texttt{education\_tr}, and 286 versus 210 on \texttt{law\_tr}---so restricting candidate generation to one model under-covers relevance within this evaluated roster. Second, the ranking was stable across the two tested constructions. Table~\ref{tbl:pooled_survival} shows that the aggregate ordering \texttt{M5}~$>$~\texttt{M3}~$>$~\texttt{M4}~$>$~\texttt{M1}~$>$~\texttt{M2} is identical under both the single-model and the model-independent pools, and the per-dataset leading patterns are likewise preserved (\texttt{M5} then \texttt{M3} on both Turkish corpora; English-oriented models leading on English). This comparison does not prove that the pool is complete or reusable for arbitrary future systems; it only shows that the observed ranking survived the two available qrels constructions.

\subsection{Qualitative Examples and Error Analysis}\label{examples-error-analysis}

To make the benchmark task concrete, consider the \texttt{education\_eng} query \emph{``What are the requirements for students to pass each level and advance in the ELS program?''}, generated from a seed chunk of the English-language-school regulations. A judged-relevant chunk describes how a student is ``placed in the next level of the one they have completed successfully,'' directly answering the question, whereas a judged-non-relevant near-miss is an admission chunk listing GCE A-Level entry grades, which is topically adjacent (levels and passing grades) but concerns admission rather than in-program progression. The Turkish examples are analogous: an \texttt{education\_tr} query asks about the university's budget and accounting principles, and a \texttt{law\_tr} query asks about the reorganization of departments and representative offices in capital-market institutions. These examples illustrate the kind of corpus-grounded synthetic queries and near-miss negative judgments produced by the pipeline; they are not a human validation study or a frequency analysis of model-specific errors.

Three observations temper and contextualize the results. First, in this snapshot each query's seed chunk is judged like any other candidate rather than force-labeled relevant; this removes the guaranteed-positive bias of a forced-seed protocol and is reflected in a high but no-longer-saturated \texttt{Success@10} on \texttt{education\_eng} (about 0.96 across models rather than 1.00). Second, the model-independent pooled qrels store explicit negative judgments alongside positives---1{,}805, 3{,}024, and 3{,}515 non-relevant labels for \texttt{education\_eng}, \texttt{education\_tr}, and \texttt{law\_tr} respectively---so non-relevant decisions are inspectable rather than discarded. Third, the examples help explain why the paper treats the quantitative rankings as preliminary decision-support evidence: without human audit or judge-agreement data, the examples can illustrate the retrieval task but cannot independently verify query realism, label reliability, or model-specific failure rates.
