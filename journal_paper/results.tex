\section{Results}\label{results}

This section reports the evidence produced by the proposed benchmarking pipeline under the fixed experimental setup described in Section~\ref{experiments}. The purpose of the reported results is practical model selection for local corpora rather than universal leaderboard comparison. Because the pipeline generates full benchmark reports automatically and dynamically, only a selected subset of the most decision-relevant tables and figures is included here. The full automatically generated reports and supporting artifacts are available through the public repository for interested readers. \citep{GitHub_RagRetrievalBench}

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Headline Findings}\label{cross-dataset-headline-findings}

The fixed three-dataset snapshot yields three immediate findings. First, Table~\ref{tbl:cross_dataset_consistency_summary} shows that the multilingual mpnet model (\texttt{M5}) is the strongest overall candidate by average rank and mean \texttt{MAP@10}; Section~\ref{robustness-pooled-qrels} confirms that this ordering persists when the relevance judgments are rebuilt from a single-model rather than a model-independent candidate pool. Second, Table~\ref{tbl:dataset_result_summary} shows that retrieval difficulty is driven mainly by language: \texttt{education\_eng} is by far the easiest benchmark (mean \texttt{MAP@10} 0.45), whereas the two Turkish corpora are markedly harder and of similar difficulty to each other (0.21 each). Third, Figure~\ref{fig:results_cross_dataset_performance} shows that the English benchmark sits at a higher absolute score level, while the Turkish benchmarks are lower and separate the roster mainly by model family. Taken together, these shared-protocol artifacts show that the pipeline can turn heterogeneous local corpora into one directly comparable decision-support view, which is the paper's main answer to RQ1. The aggregate diagnostics in Table~\ref{tbl:cross_dataset_consistency_summary} extend that answer by showing that the workflow also produces robustness evidence, not only point estimates: the same benchmark can identify models that are strong on average, models that degrade sharply under language shift, and models whose behavior is comparatively stable across the full metric set.

Table~\ref{tbl:cross_dataset_consistency_summary} connects the aggregate ranking to the paper's language and domain claims. Average rank and mean \texttt{MAP@10} identify strong aggregate performers, while three consistency diagnostics summarize how stable each model is across the six retrieval metrics and three dataset conditions. For a given model, let $S$ be its set of scores over all six metrics and all three datasets; the \emph{all-metric CV} is $\mathrm{std}(S)/\mathrm{mean}(S)$ (relative variability, lower is more stable), the \emph{worst drop} is $(\max S-\min S)/\max S$ (the largest relative fall from the model's best to its worst score), and the \emph{score range} is $\max S-\min S$ (the same gap in absolute terms). This speaks to RQ2 and RQ3 within the snapshot: the largest drops occur when model behavior interacts with corpus language---the English-oriented models are competitive on \texttt{education\_eng} but far less stable across the Turkish conditions---whereas \texttt{M5} combines the best average rank with the smallest consistency penalty (\texttt{CV}~0.41) in this snapshot.

\subsection{Dataset-Level Results: education\_eng}\label{results-education-eng}

The English education subset produces the strongest retrieval scores in the present paper. Table~\ref{tbl:education_eng_metrics} shows that the three English-trained models (\texttt{M1}, \texttt{M2}, and \texttt{M4}) lead, with \texttt{M1} reaching the highest \texttt{MAP@10} at 0.5260, \texttt{M2} close behind at 0.5022, and \texttt{M4} a little lower at 0.4587; the multilingual models \texttt{M5} (0.4246) and \texttt{M3} (0.3557) trail. Figure~\ref{fig:results_education_eng_model_comparison} reinforces this English-favors-monolingual pattern across the full metric profile.

Table~\ref{tbl:education_eng_timing} shows that \texttt{M1} is the standout choice here: it combines the highest \texttt{MAP@10} with the lowest query latency at 13.10 ms/query. \texttt{M2} matches its quality closely (0.5022) but at roughly three times the latency (42.62 ms/query), while \texttt{M4} is a slightly lower-quality, low-latency alternative (0.4587 at 24.31 ms/query). For English education corpora, the benchmark therefore points to \texttt{M1} as both the quality-first and latency-first choice, with \texttt{M4} as a lightweight alternative and \texttt{M2} defensible only when its marginally higher \texttt{MRR@10} matters more than its higher cost.

\subsection{Language Effect}\label{language-effect}

Under the shared evaluation setup in Section~\ref{shared-evaluation-setup}, the English and Turkish education corpora provide a within-snapshot comparison of whether model behavior changes with corpus language. Because the comparison rests on a single corpus per language, language here is confounded with corpus identity, so the effect below is a bounded case observation rather than an isolated language effect. Table~\ref{tbl:language_effect_comparison} shows that the English results sit in a relatively narrow high band, with all five models between 0.3557 and 0.5260 in \texttt{MAP@10}, whereas the Turkish results are both lower and more spread by model family.

The monolingual English-oriented models (\texttt{M1}, \texttt{M2}, and \texttt{M4}) all decline substantially on \texttt{education\_tr}: \texttt{M1} drops by 0.4029, \texttt{M2} by 0.4082, and \texttt{M4} by 0.2733. By contrast, the multilingual models are far more stable across languages, each dropping only about 0.06 (\texttt{M3} by $0.0585$ and \texttt{M5} by $0.0640$). The evidence therefore suggests a language-level family tendency within this snapshot rather than a universal rule, and it also shows that \texttt{M1} leads \texttt{education\_eng}, so an English-trained model can still be the strong choice when the corpus language matches the model family.

The practical implication is straightforward. For local retrieval deployment, language should be treated as a first-order selection variable: the best model for an English corpus is not necessarily the best model for a Turkish corpus, even when both corpora are evaluated under the same benchmark workflow. That is the main value of this result for the paper's fit-for-purpose claim.

\subsection{Quality-Speed Tradeoff}\label{quality-speed-tradeoff}

Table~\ref{tbl:quality_speed_summary} combines aggregate quality and timing evidence, and Figure~\ref{fig:results_quality_speed_tradeoff} makes the same tradeoff visible as a two-dimensional ranking. The points separate into three useful regimes. \texttt{M5} occupies the quality-leading end of the plot with the highest mean \texttt{MAP@10} at 0.3810, but it does so at a weighted latency of 42.94 ms/query and a throughput range of 13.3--13.9 chunks/s. \texttt{M1} anchors the speed-leading end with the lowest weighted latency at 16.28 ms/query and the highest throughput range of 42.3--51.3 chunks/s, but its mean \texttt{MAP@10} is only 0.2536. \texttt{M2} is the weakest aggregate choice in this snapshot because it combines the lowest mean \texttt{MAP@10} (0.2408) with the highest weighted latency (51.66 ms/query) and the slowest throughput range (3.5--7.7 chunks/s).

Within this roster, the multilingual models \texttt{M3} and \texttt{M5} provide the strongest cross-dataset retrieval quality, while low latency is concentrated in the lightweight MiniLM models (\texttt{M1}, and to a lesser extent \texttt{M3} and \texttt{M4}); speed is not a pure family effect, because the monolingual mpnet model \texttt{M2} is the slowest model overall. This pattern should not be treated as universal: \texttt{M1} leads \texttt{education\_eng}, showing that an English-trained model can still be an effective choice when the corpus and deployment priority align.

The middle of the plot contains \texttt{M3} and \texttt{M4}, the most plausible compromise candidates when neither extreme is acceptable. \texttt{M3} is the stronger of the two on absolute retrieval quality, with mean \texttt{MAP@10} of 0.3051 versus 0.2799, at a similar latency (25.05 ms/query versus 26.91), and the two share an average rank of 3.00. The practical reading is therefore straightforward: \texttt{M5} is the best choice when retrieval effectiveness matters most, \texttt{M1} is the best choice when latency and indexing throughput dominate, and \texttt{M3} is the best-balanced compromise for deployments that need an intermediate quality-speed profile. On this evidence, no single model dominates all others jointly; the preferred embedding model depends on whether the practitioner values retrieval effectiveness, indexing cost, or interactive response time more strongly.

\subsection{Statistical Decision Support}\label{statistical-decision-support}

The benchmark is intended not only to rank embedding models but to indicate whether observed differences are distinguishable from sampling noise. Comparing models with a Friedman test or paired $t$-tests over only three dataset-level means would be underpowered and easy to over-read. We therefore report query-level uncertainty instead. Treating the 150 queries as the unit of analysis, we compute percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals for each model's aggregate \texttt{MAP@10} ($B=10{,}000$ resamples) and paired bootstrap intervals with two-sided bootstrap $p$-values for the difference between \texttt{M5} and each other model. Table~\ref{tbl:bootstrap_ci} and Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap_forest} report the result for the snapshot's pooled qrels.

The bootstrap layer is therefore advisory, snapshot-bounded decision support rather than a universal significance claim, and it replaces the earlier three-point test as the paper's inferential evidence.

\subsection{Robustness to Candidate-Pool Construction}\label{robustness-pooled-qrels}

The relevance judgments used throughout this paper are built from a model-independent candidate pool: for each query the top-20 candidates retrieved by all five models are unioned, deduplicated, and judged by the LLM ($9{,}296$ query--chunk pairs across the three datasets, with explicit positive and negative labels). This design deliberately prevents any single evaluated model from shaping the ground truth. A natural concern is whether the reported ranking is sensitive to this choice---in particular, whether \texttt{M5}'s lead is an artifact of how the pool is built. To probe this, we compare against a single-model candidate pool that uses only \texttt{M5}, an evaluated model, to retrieve the candidates, which was the construction used in the earlier version of this snapshot.

Two findings follow. First, the model-independent pool surfaces substantially more relevant chunks than the single-model pool---312 versus 215 positives on \texttt{education\_eng}, 354 versus 199 on \texttt{education\_tr}, and 286 versus 210 on \texttt{law\_tr}---so restricting candidate generation to one model under-covers relevance. Second, and decisively for the paper's claims, the model ranking is stable across the two constructions. Table~\ref{tbl:pooled_survival} shows that the aggregate ordering \texttt{M5}~$>$~\texttt{M3}~$>$~\texttt{M4}~$>$~\texttt{M1}~$>$~\texttt{M2} is identical under both the single-model and the model-independent pools, and the per-dataset top two are likewise preserved (\texttt{M5} then \texttt{M3} on both Turkish corpora; monolingual models leading on English). Under the model-independent pool---where \texttt{M5} has no privileged role---\texttt{M5} remains the strongest aggregate model, and its advantage over \texttt{M3} is significant at the query level ($p<0.001$; Table~\ref{tbl:bootstrap_ci}). The headline findings are therefore not artifacts of the candidate-pool construction; removing \texttt{M5}'s privileged role leaves its lead intact.

\subsection{Qualitative Examples and Error Analysis}\label{examples-error-analysis}

To make the benchmark task concrete, consider the \texttt{education\_eng} query \emph{``What are the requirements for students to pass each level and advance in the ELS program?''}, generated from a seed chunk of the English-language-school regulations. A judged-relevant chunk describes how a student is ``placed in the next level of the one they have completed successfully,'' directly answering the question, whereas a judged-non-relevant near-miss is an admission chunk listing GCE A-Level entry grades, which is topically adjacent (levels and passing grades) but concerns admission rather than in-program progression. The Turkish examples are analogous: an \texttt{education\_tr} query asks about the university's budget and accounting principles, and a \texttt{law\_tr} query asks about the reorganization of departments and representative offices in capital-market institutions. These illustrate that the synthetic queries are corpus-grounded and that the negative judgments are plausible near-misses rather than random distractors.

Three observations temper and contextualize the results. First, in this snapshot each query's seed chunk is judged like any other candidate rather than force-labeled relevant; this removes the guaranteed-positive bias of a forced-seed protocol and is reflected in a high but no-longer-saturated \texttt{Success@10} on \texttt{education\_eng} (about 0.96 across models rather than 1.00). Second, the model-independent pooled qrels store explicit negative judgments alongside positives---1{,}805, 3{,}024, and 3{,}515 non-relevant labels for \texttt{education\_eng}, \texttt{education\_tr}, and \texttt{law\_tr} respectively---so non-relevant decisions are auditable rather than discarded. Third, the largest errors come from the English-trained models on the Turkish corpora (\texttt{M1}, \texttt{M2}), which tend to retrieve lexically similar but semantically off-target chunks; this is the failure mode the multilingual models avoid and the main driver of the language effect reported above.
