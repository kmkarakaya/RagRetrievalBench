\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

This paper presented a reusable local benchmarking pipeline for selecting embedding models under corpus-specific RAG requirements. The pipeline combines document ingestion, chunking, synthetic query generation, qrels construction, multi-model retrieval evaluation, and automated reporting within one fixed-protocol workflow. Its main contribution is not a new universal embedding leaderboard, but a practical decision-support process: practitioners can evaluate candidate models on their own document collections, preserve the raw benchmark evidence, and regenerate dataset-level or aggregate reports that combine retrieval quality, operational cost, consistency diagnostics, and statistical context. \citep{GitHub_RagRetrievalBench}

The fixed experimental snapshot demonstrates that the pipeline produces actionable evidence for local model selection, directly addressing RQ1. Across three datasets, five embedding models, six retrieval metrics, and timing measurements, the reports identify both dataset-specific winners and cross-dataset tradeoffs. In aggregate, \texttt{M5} is the strongest quality-first candidate in this snapshot, with the best average rank and highest mean \texttt{MAP@10}. At the same time, the Results section shows why this ranking should be interpreted as deployment evidence rather than as a universal claim: \texttt{M1} is the best \texttt{MAP@10} performer on \texttt{education\_eng} (and also its lowest-latency model), \texttt{M5} leads both Turkish datasets, and \texttt{M1} provides the lowest weighted query latency overall. The aggregate advantage of \texttt{M5} is significant at the query level (bootstrap $p<0.001$ against every other model) and, importantly, holds under a model-independent candidate pool in which \texttt{M5} has no privileged role, so it is not an artifact of the qrels construction.

The language and domain analyses answer RQ2 and RQ3 by showing that embedding behavior changes materially when the corpus changes, even under a shared benchmark protocol. In the education domain, the English corpus places the English-trained models at the top, whereas the Turkish corpus separates the multilingual models clearly from the rest of the roster. Within Turkish, however, the shift from education to law/regulation changes retrieval performance and rankings only marginally, so in this snapshot language rather than domain is the dominant selection factor. These findings support the paper's central claim that local benchmarking is necessary for fit-for-purpose selection: model choice should be conditioned first on corpus language, and on domain and document structure where these matter, rather than transferred directly from a generic benchmark or from another local dataset.

The quality-speed and statistical decision-support results answer RQ4. The benchmark exposes practical operating regimes rather than a single dominant model. \texttt{M5} is preferable when retrieval effectiveness is the main priority, \texttt{M1} is preferable when low latency dominates, and \texttt{M3} or \texttt{M4} are more plausible compromise candidates when both quality and response time matter. The statistical outputs add an uncertainty-aware layer to this interpretation. Rather than testing over three dataset means, the pipeline reports query-level bootstrap confidence intervals; these confirm \texttt{M5}'s aggregate advantage while exposing wide, overlapping per-model intervals and a non-uniform ordering across conditions, since the monolingual models lead on the English corpus. The pipeline's value therefore lies in making both the descriptive ranking and its evidential limits visible to the practitioner.

The claims of this paper remain bounded to the reported snapshot: three local datasets, five sentence-transformer models, one fixed retrieval configuration, 150 synthetic queries, and 952 positive relevance judgments in the frozen snapshot. The education datasets and law/regulation dataset were collected from the documented institutional and regulatory sources, and the implementation is publicly available for inspection and reuse. \citep{TED_Regulations,BDDK_Mevzuat,GitHub_RagRetrievalBench}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations bound how the reported evidence should be used. First, the empirical scope is small and confounded: three datasets, five open-weight sentence-transformer models, and one retrieval configuration, with language and domain tied to dataset identity (one English and one Turkish education corpus, and one Turkish legal corpus of only two source documents). The language- and domain-conditioned effects are therefore within-snapshot case observations, not population-level claims. Second, the queries are synthetic and the relevance labels are produced by an LLM judge; this paper does not include a human audit or human--LLM agreement, so query realism and judgment reliability are not independently validated, and a sampled human-audit protocol is left to future work. Because each query is generated from a corpus chunk (although that seed chunk is now judged like any other candidate rather than forced relevant), the task may still be easier than real user information needs, consistent with the high \texttt{Success@10} (about 0.96) on the English corpus. Third, the statistical support is descriptive: the query-level bootstrap intervals quantify sampling variability within this snapshot but do not license generalization beyond it. Fourth, the timing measurements were collected on a single CPU machine (recorded in each run's environment snapshot) and should be read as relative, same-machine comparisons rather than absolute latencies. Addressing these limitations, rather than the pipeline mechanics, is the main work required before the reported rankings could be treated as deployment guarantees.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{future-work}

Future work should extend the pipeline in four directions. First, the benchmark should be expanded to additional Turkish, English, and multilingual corpora so that language and domain effects can be studied over more than three dataset-level observations. Second, the reporting layer should include sensitivity analyses for chunk size, overlap, candidate-pool size, query count, and retrieval cutoff, because these parameters may change the model ranking for some deployment settings. Third, the qrels construction stage should incorporate human-audit protocols and judge-calibration diagnostics to quantify the reliability of synthetic relevance labels. Fourth, the model roster should be broadened beyond the five sentence-transformer baselines used here, including newer multilingual, domain-adapted, and commercial embedding models when licensing and deployment constraints allow.

Overall, the evidence supports the paper's practical claim: a controlled local benchmark can make embedding selection more transparent, reproducible, and application-specific. The results also show why such a benchmark should report more than a single score. For realistic RAG deployment, practitioners need to see retrieval effectiveness, language and domain sensitivity, operational cost, consistency behavior, and statistical uncertainty together before selecting an embedding model for a local corpus.
