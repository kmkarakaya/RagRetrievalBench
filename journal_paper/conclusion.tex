\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

This paper presented a reusable, configuration-driven local benchmarking pipeline for corpus-specific embedding-model selection in RAG retrieval. The workflow processes each document collection separately through ingestion, chunking, synthetic query generation, pooled qrels construction, multi-model retrieval evaluation, timing capture, and automated reporting. Its central research position is deliberately bounded: practitioners should not assume that one embedding model will be optimal for every corpus under their control. The three-corpus snapshot demonstrates how the workflow tests that assumption; it does not prove that a universal best model cannot exist \citep{GitHub_RagRetrievalBench}.

For RQ1, the fixed snapshot shows that the configurable pipeline can produce inspectable, corpus-specific decision-support evidence. Separate reports for \texttt{education\_eng}, \texttt{education\_tr}, and \texttt{law\_tr} contain comparable retrieval metrics, timing summaries, query-level descriptive uncertainty, and preserved artifacts under the same five-model protocol. These reports support corpus-specific screening rather than a single cross-corpus selection rule.

For RQ2, the two education cases show language-associated ranking differences: English-oriented candidates lead \texttt{education\_eng}, whereas multilingual candidates lead \texttt{education\_tr}. This bounded comparison does not establish a causal language effect because each language condition is represented by one corpus. For RQ3, the two Turkish cases instead show domain-associated stability: \texttt{M5} remains the quality-first candidate and \texttt{M3} the lower-latency alternative for both \texttt{education\_tr} and \texttt{law\_tr}. This stability does not show that domain is irrelevant; together, RQ2 and RQ3 demonstrate why both shortlist change and shortlist stability must be measured through separate corpus runs.

For RQ4, the reports show that retrieval quality and operational priorities can produce different corpus-specific selections. They combine retrieval metrics with compound corpus embedding/index-insertion throughput, compound query-embedding/vector-search latency, and descriptive uncertainty intervals. In the demonstrated scenario, the corpus-level evidence points to \texttt{M1} for \texttt{education\_eng} on latency, its quality advantage over \texttt{M2} being within the paired bootstrap interval; to \texttt{M5} for quality-first Turkish use, where the advantage over the runner-up is separated; and to \texttt{M3} as the lower-latency Turkish alternative. The aggregate performance of \texttt{M5} remains secondary comparative context and does not override these corpus-level readings.

The current limitations define focused directions for strengthening the reliability and breadth of corpus-specific recommendations. Limited validation of synthetic queries and LLM-generated qrels calls for human audit and judge calibration; the fixed experimental configuration calls for controlled sensitivity studies through the existing configuration interface; bounded corpus and model coverage calls for additional matched corpora that examine language- and domain-associated differences or stability, broader embedding-model rosters, and contextual retrieval baselines; incomplete operational evidence calls for repeated measurements of timing variability, peak memory, and model size on declared hardware; and mutable reproducibility artifacts call for immutable archived releases containing configurations, prompts, artifacts, checksums, and environment metadata.

The practical takeaway is that no universally best embedding model should be assumed: practitioners should evaluate each materially distinct corpus separately, treat aggregate results as secondary context, and select according to corpus-specific evidence and project priorities.
