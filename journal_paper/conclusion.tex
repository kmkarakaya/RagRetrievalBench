\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

This paper presented a reusable, configuration-driven local benchmarking pipeline whose central purpose is corpus-specific embedding-model selection for RAG retrieval, demonstrated through one frozen experimental instantiation. The completed workflow processes each document collection separately through ingestion, chunking, synthetic query generation, pooled qrels construction, multi-model retrieval evaluation, timing capture, and automated reporting. The results support a practical conclusion: a practitioner should benchmark each materially distinct corpus rather than transfer one generic or aggregate winner automatically across collections. Dataset-level reports are therefore the primary selection artifacts; aggregate summaries provide secondary cross-corpus context. \citep{GitHub_RagRetrievalBench}

RQ1 asked whether the pipeline can produce decision-support evidence for embedding-model selection on each local document collection. The answer is yes within the fixed snapshot: the pipeline produced separate dataset reports for \texttt{education\_eng}, \texttt{education\_tr}, and \texttt{law\_tr}, with comparable retrieval metrics, timing summaries, query-level descriptive uncertainty, and preserved artifacts under the same five-model protocol. These reports support corpus-specific screening rather than a single cross-corpus deployment rule.

RQ2 asked whether the pipeline can expose language-conditioned ranking changes within this bounded snapshot. The answer is that the two education cases show different model-family rankings: English-oriented candidates lead the English education corpus, while multilingual candidates lead the Turkish education corpus. This is a language-associated case observation, not a causal language estimate, because each language condition is represented by one corpus.

RQ3 asked whether the pipeline can expose domain-associated ranking differences or stability within this bounded snapshot. The answer is that the two Turkish cases show stability rather than a major ranking change: \texttt{M5} remains the quality-first candidate and \texttt{M3} remains the lower-latency compromise for both \texttt{education\_tr} and \texttt{law\_tr}. This does not show that domain is irrelevant; it shows that a separate corpus run can reveal when a shortlist is preserved under the completed protocol.

RQ4 asked how the pipeline combines retrieval effectiveness, operational cost, and query-level uncertainty into fit-for-purpose guidance. The answer is that the reports combine retrieval metrics with compound corpus embedding/index-insertion throughput, compound query-embedding/vector-search latency, and descriptive uncertainty intervals so that different corpus and priority choices remain visible. In the observed snapshot, \texttt{M1} is the appropriate candidate for \texttt{education\_eng} when both \texttt{MAP@10} and latency are prioritized; \texttt{M5} is the quality-first choice for the Turkish corpora; and \texttt{M3} is the lower-latency Turkish compromise. The aggregate \texttt{M5} result is useful comparative context, but it does not override those corpus-level readings.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Several limitations bound how the reported evidence should be used. The empirical scope contains one corpus per language/domain condition, so language and domain are tied to corpus identity, source selection, document structure, generated queries, and qrels composition. The query set is synthetic, the relevance labels are produced by an LLM judge, and the paper includes no human validation or human--LLM agreement analysis. The experiment generated 150 queries, but statistical scoring used 148 positive-bearing queries after zero-positive exclusion. The reported experimental instantiation used a fixed retrieval configuration: default non-normalized Sentence-Transformers embeddings were searched with ChromaDB squared-Euclidean (L2) distance, with no normalized-cosine or model-recommended similarity sensitivity analysis. The pipeline's ability to accept alternative configurations does not establish how the rankings would change under them.

The roster and evidence base are intentionally bounded. The five evaluated Sentence-Transformers candidates do not include lexical retrieval, hybrid retrieval, reranking, domain-adapted embeddings, newer multilingual models, or commercial alternatives. The chunk size, overlap, 50-query target, top-20 pool depth, and \texttt{k=10} cutoff are demonstration settings rather than tuned optima. The qrels are roster-conditional because the judged pool is built from candidates retrieved by the evaluated models, so relevant chunks missed by every pooled system may remain unjudged. Operational measurements are compound, single-run, single-machine CPU observations: embedding throughput includes corpus embedding plus index insertion, query latency includes query embedding plus vector search, and runtime memory was not measured. Finally, the public repository supports inspection and reuse, but it is a mutable implementation and artifact source rather than an immutable archived release with fixed service behavior and permanent identifiers.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{future-work}

Future work should add evidence rather than treating the present wording as a substitute for it. Larger and more deliberately matched corpus collections are needed to study language- and domain-associated behavior beyond the three worked cases. Human audits and judge-calibration studies are needed to assess synthetic-query realism and LLM qrels quality. Protocol sensitivity studies should use the pipeline's configurable settings to examine retrieval geometry, chunking, query count, pool depth, retrieval cutoff, and repeated timing; operational extensions should separate embedding from index insertion, separate query embedding from vector search, measure runtime memory, and report variability across hardware or repeated runs. The evaluated roster should also be expanded to lexical, hybrid, reranking, domain-adapted, newer multilingual, and commercial alternatives where the deployment setting permits. A tagged or archived release with frozen prompts, artifacts, checksums, service dates, and environment details would strengthen long-term reproducibility.

Overall, the evidence supports a bounded practical lesson: a controlled local benchmark can make corpus-specific embedding selection more transparent and inspectable, but the preferred candidate should be read from the report for the corpus and priority at hand. In this snapshot, that means \texttt{M1} for \texttt{education\_eng}, \texttt{M5} for Turkish quality-first use, and \texttt{M3} as the lower-latency Turkish compromise, with aggregate \texttt{M5} performance retained only as secondary context.
